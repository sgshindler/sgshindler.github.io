Tensors are a notoriously confusing topic, and they are a concept with enough applications in physics, engineering and mathematics that it's unrealistic to try and "explain what a tensor is". After reading this post, you should have a good conceptual idea of what a tensor is and why they're different from matrices or arrays. I'm hoping to give you a sense of what a tensor is the same way you know what an automobile is - you know it gets you places and it's not a bicycle, but you might not be able to fix the engine. First, I'm going to build up the conceptual idea, and how its distinguished from matrices. Next, I'm going to use a few examples to show how tensors arise in (1) coordinate transformations (2) continuum mechanics (3) large collections of particles. These examples cover most reasons why you might need to use tensors in an engineering or physics context. Tensors also show up in electromagnetism and quantum mechanics, but I'm not aware of a really good *reason* why, so... sorry. I'll finish off by talking about how this math is generalized. This generalization is what makes tensors *really* powerful, so I'll include a few resources for further investigation. I'm going to keep this discussion pretty qualitative, and I'm not going to talk about topics like covariance and contravariance.

# Vectors and Matrices store numbers

First, I need to talk about matrices, but I want to bring as little baggage as possible into this discussion, so forget about matrix multiplication and determinants and just think of matrices as arrays that store numbers. In this case we'll work with real-valued matrices, but matrices could also contain complex numbers. Those are important, but not important right now. You've probably seen vectors before, probably represented as a list of numbers. In this case the length of the vector **v** is the dimension of the vector space it lives in $$ \mathbb{R}^3 $$ in the same way that a scalar variable x might live in $$\mathbb{R}$$. You might write **v** as,

$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

where $$v_1$$, $$v_2$$ and $$v_3$$ are the components of the vector. A matrix is essentially a vector of vectors. In 3-dimensions, you might write a matrix as,

$$\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} M_{11} & M_{12} & M_{13} \\ M_{21} & M_{22} & M_{23} \\ M_{31} M_{32} M_{33} \end{bmatrix}$$

In this post I'm going to use two simple matrix operations, addition, and scalar multiplication. If you want to add two matrices or vectors together, they need to be the same size. When you add them, you just add their elements together directly as they're presented.

$$\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1+u_1 \\ v_2+u_2 \\ v_3+u_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

If you want to multiply by a scalar, you multiply all the elements of the vector or matrix by the scalar number. 

$$2\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 v_1 \\ 2 v_2 \\ 2 v_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Finally, you can easily imagine having vectors of matrices, or matrices of matrices. Here is a vector of matrices:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} M_{111} & M_{121} & M_{131} \\ M_{211} & M_{221} & M_{231} \\ M_{311} M_{321} M_{331} \end{bmatrix}$$ \\ \begin{bmatrix} M_{112} & M_{122} & M_{132} \\ M_{212} & M_{222} & M_{232} \\ M_{312} M_{322} M_{332} \end{bmatrix}$$ \\ \begin{bmatrix} M_{113} & M_{123} & M_{133} \\ M_{213} & M_{223} & M_{233} \\ M_{313} M_{323} M_{333} \end{bmatrix}$$ \end{bmatrix}$$

Most people generalize the idea of a vector of matrices as a 3D cube of numbers - analogizing the vector to a line and the matrix to a flat square. This stops working at 4 dimensions, since most people can't visualize a 4D object. For these higher order matrices, don't visualize a cube of numbers, instead visualize a vector of matrices or a matrix of matrices. You'll be able to visualize higher dimensional data better and as a result, you will be more confident working with them. You can also have non-square matrices, which have a different number of dimensions on one side than the other. Organizing numbers into arrays like this is really useful for storing information. My sense is that most people who take linear algebra feel that the idea of a matrix is relatively intuitive and I hope that is true of the short introduction I've given here. However, those same people often find the difference between a matrix and a tensor confusing and nuanced - in the next section I'm going to try and fix that.

# I lied. Vectors are not lists of numbers. I'm really sorry.

I've just said that a vector is a list of numbers, and that's not totally true. The word vector refers to multiple things which often get conflated. One of those things is a list of numbers, the other is, for lack of a better word, an arrow.

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig1.png)

An arrow is clearly *not* a list of numbers. But if you want to do math with arrows, you need to find a numerical way to write them out. The simplest way to do that is to add a coordinate system. For my purposes, vectors will always originate at the origin of the coordinate system. If you want to learn how to do math on vectors which do not originate at the origin, I would strongly recommend sudgylacmoe on YouTube. Specifically his video on projective geometric algebra, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0i3ocLhbxJ4&t=507s. Textbooks are also a good option, since projective geometric algebra may not be the default language used by others. 

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig2.png)

Next, I'm going to define a basis. A basis (in 2D) consists of two orthogonal vectors with a length of 1. I'll call the one which points in the 'x' direction $$\mathbf{e_1}$$ and the one in the 'y' direction $$\mathbf{e_2}$$. We can multiply these basis vectors by scalar numbers and add them together to get any vector in our 2D vector space. For instance, you might have,

$$ \mathbf{v} = v_1*\mathbf{e_1}+v_2*\mathbf{e_2} $$

which is graphically represented by the image:

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig3.png)

Often, the unit vectors are dropped, which recovers the framing of the vector as a list of numbers.

$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The unit vectors are often unimportant, but the basis vectors I chose were arbitrary, so I could have ended up with a totally different list of numbers if I chose a different basis.

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig4.png)

Despite the ubiquitous representation of vectors as lists of numbers, arrows are clearly *not* lists of numbers. They're represented that way because, once you choose a set of basis vectors, you can represent an arrow as a list of numbers. In short, tensors generalize that idea to matrices. You can represent a tensor as a matrix *only if you've chosen a basis*. This idea is really the key idea in distinguishing a matrix from a tensor, but I've never found it to be a very satisfying explanation on its own, so I'm going to keep going. 

First, if a vector has basis vectors, then a tensor has basis tensors. Basis tensors are formed by combining basis vectors using an operation known as the tensor product or direct product. The tensor product of two basis vectors $$\mathbf{e_1}$$ and $$\mathbf{e_2}$$ is given by, $$\mathbf{E_12} = \mathbf{e_1} \otimes \mathbf{e_2}$$ and importantly, the tensor product is non-commutative, so $$\mathbf{e_1} \otimes \mathbf{e_2} \neq \mathbf{e_2}\otimes \mathbf{e_1}$$. If I took the tensor product of two different vectors, I would get:

$$\bm{v} \otimes \bm{u} = v_1 u_1 \bm{e_1\otimes e_1}+v_1 u_2 \bm{e_1\otimes e_2}+v_2 u_1 \bm{e_2\otimes e_1}+v_2 u_2 \bm{e_2\otimes e_2}

Often, when writing the tensor product the $$\otimes$$ symbol is ommited, so the above expression could also be written as: 

$$\bm{v} \bm{u} = v_1 u_1 \bm{e_1 e_1}+v_1 u_2 \bm{e_1 e_2}+v_2 u_1 \bm{e_2 e_1}+v_2 u_2 \bm{e_2 e_2}$$

These tensors are 2nd order because their basis tensors are the direct product of two basis vectors, a vector would be a 1st order tensor, and a scalar number would be a 0th order tensor. A general 2nd order tensor (also called a dyad or dyadic tensor) in 2D can be written as:

$$\bm{T} = T_{11} \bm{e_1 e_1} + T_{12} \bm{e_1 e_2} + T_{21} \bm{e_2 e_1} + T_{22} \bm{e_2 e_2}$$

or

$$\bm{T} = T_{11} \bm{E_11} + T_{12} \bm{E_12} + T_{21} \bm{E_21} + T_{22} \bm{E_22}$$

If I make the first basis vector correspond to the first row / collumn of a matrix and the second basis vector to the second row / collumn, then this expression can be written as

$$\bm{T} = \begin{bmatrix} T_11 & T_12 \\ T_21 & T_22 \end{bmatrix}$$

This representation of a tensor is where the correspondence between matrices and tensors comes from. As a representation it's only really used pedagogically and when the specific values of $$T_11$$, $$T_12$$, etc. are important. I've never really had to worry about writing out numerical values for tensors, and given the broad confusion about what a tensor is, I'm not sure it's the best pedagogical tool. A *much* more useful and compact way to write out a tensor is using indicial, or Einstein notation. If you're comfortable with summation notation, you might notice that you can write out the expression for our arbitrary tensor **T** much more efficiently than I did above,

$$\bm{T} = T_{11} \bm{e_1 e_1} + T_{12} \bm{e_1 e_2} + T_{21} \bm{e_2 e_1} + T_{22} \bm{e_2 e_2} = \sum_{i=1}^{2} \sum_{j=1}^{2} T_{ij} \mathbf{e_i e_j}]$$

Since both the sum and the unit vectors are implied, I can drop the sum and the unit vectors and convey the key information about my tensor.

$$\bm{T} = T_{ij}$$

Before moving on, I want to recap the different representations of vectors and tensors.
I've written them as a set of numbers multiplied by unit vectors or unit tensors,

$$ \bm{v} = v_1*\bm{e_1}+v_2*\bm{e_2} $$, $$\bm{T} = T_{11} \bm{e_1 e_1} + T_{12} \bm{e_1 e_2} + T_{21} \bm{e_2 e_1} + T_{22} \bm{e_2 e_2}$$

as a list of numbers or a matrix,

$$\bm{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$$ , $$\bm{T} = \begin{bmatrix} T_11 & T_12 \\ T_21 & T_22 \end{bmatrix}$$

using index notation,

$$v_i$$, $$T_ij$$

and graphically.

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig5.png)

If a vector is an arrow, then what is a tensor?? All the mathematical abstractions I gave related to vectors were tools allowing me to describe an arrow symbolically. There is no tensor analogy to drawing a vector as an arrow - you kindof just have to think of it as a weighted sum of basis tensors. The way to resolve the tension between a vector being an arrow, and a tensor being a weighted sum of basis tensors is *not* to find a graphical representation of a tensor. Graphical representations of tensors are invaluable, but they're only useful within a specific context, so it would be wrong to claim that they really describe what a tensor *is*. Instead, try and get comfortable thinking about vectors *more* abstractly. If a vector becomes a weighted sum of basis vectors, then you open the door to a vector being something other than an arrow in space. That is one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics.

# Why do we need to use tensors?

Hopefully you know what a tensor is now. But when they show up in a physics or engineering context, the justification for them can be difficult to rationalize. If you've heard that tensors are objects which map one vector onto another vector, then why do they describe stress in fluids? Any discussion of "what a tensor is" would be incomplete without showing how different uses of tensors are connected to each other. This post is getting a little long, so I'm going to keep this last part a little shorter.

## Tensors as coordinate transformations

So far, I haven't talked about multiplying tensors. The only multiplication you really need is the dot product. I'm going to use a coordinate system with orthogonal unit vectors, which are Euclidean. These kinds of restrictions can be relaxed later. The dot product is defined so that the dot product of two orthogonal unit vectors is 0 (orthogonal), and two co-linear unit vectors is 1 (Euclidean). This is described by the following equations,

$$e_1 \cdot e_2 = e_2 \cdot e_1 = 0, e_1 \cdot e_1 = 1, e_2 \cdot e_2 = 1$$

Therefore, if you were two take the dot product of two vectors, you would get the following.

$$v \cdot u = v_1 u_1 e_1 \cdot e_1 + v_1 u_2 e_1 \cdot e_2 + v_2 u_1 e_2 \cdot e_1 + v_2 u_2 e_2 \cdot e_2 = v_1 u_1 + v_2 u_2 = v_i u_i

As we can see, the dot product in indicial notation is represented by repeated indices. This is the way I will represent it moving forward. If this is the first time you're seeing the dot product, I would recommend looking into other resources, this explanation is really just intended for completeness. I'm going to try and show that a tensor is fundamentally a coordinate transformation by considering the vector recovered by multiplying a tensor and a vector.

$$ v_i = u_jT_ji $$

I hope it is clear from this expression that a 2nd order tensor maps one vector onto another vector - u onto v. When $$u = e_1$$, then $$v_i = T_1i$$, and when $$u = e_2$$, then $$v_i = T_2i$$. $$T_1i$$ and $$T_2i$$ are the first and second rows of the tensor T respectively. They are totally independent vectors and can be defined arbitrarily. What T is doing here is transforming the orthogonal unit vector basis onto a new, totally arbitrary one. This is shown in the following drawing.

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig6.png)
![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig7.png)

There are two ways to interpret this transformation. The first interpretation is that we're simply transforming u into a new vector v, and that the basis vectors are staying the same. However, I could also consider that this transformation is changing the basis, and that - in reality u and v describe the same vector but with a different basis. Once you understand that an arbitrary tensor can be thought of as an arbitrary change of basis, a number of questions begin to arise. What kind of transformations preserve handedness of the basis? What kind of tensor describes a rotation? What kind of tensor preserves the length of a vector? Answering these questions are fundamental and critial to developing a deeper understanding of tensors and how they're used.

## The Stress Tensor

The derivation of the stress tensor in fluid mechanics courses is the most intuitive discussion of a tensor that I've ever seen, so its a good explanation to start with here. Normally fluids are 3D, but I'm going to show this for a 2D fluid instead so it's easy to represent graphically. Start off by imagining a small square of fluid - one so small enough that it falls in the linear limit, so that when you think about forces on the square, the forces on each face of the square are constant. Stress is the forces within a material. Since this square has differential dimensions, the difference between the force on one side of the cube and the force on the other must be differential as well, otherwise the cube would be experiencing a finite force over a infinitesimal distance - and that would cause an infinite stress gradient, corresponding to discontinuous stress. Therefore, when the stress is assumed to vary smoothly, the forces on one side of the square must be equal and opposite of the forces on the opposite side of the cube. Next, there are two kinds of forces on the cube, normal forces and tangential forces. These are shown in the following graphic,

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig8.png)

This image can be used to derive a stress tensor by considering how to classify each of the forces in the image. There is a x-directed force on the x-directed face of the cube, a y-directed force on the y-directed face of the cube, an x-directed force on the y-directed face of the cube and a y-directed force on the x-directed face of the cube. These numbers can be represented by a tensor.

$$\bm{\sigma} = \sigma _{xx} \bm{e_x e_x} + \sigma _{xy} \bm{e_x e_y} + \sigma _{yx} \bm{e_y e_x} + \sigma _{yy} \bm{e_y e_y}$$

The next step here is to focus only on the tangential stresses. Imagine that the tangential stress is zero on one side and non-zero on the other.

![Image not available](https://sgshindler.github.io/images/blog_posts/20260530_fig9.png)

This force is a torque, and would act to rotate the cube. This internal torque would have a tendency to cause the fluid particle to rotate, which would result in discontinuities in the fluid. To prevent this internal rotation, the tangential stresses should balance each other. Therefore, the stress tensor is "symmetric", meaning that $$\sigma _ij = \sigma _ji$$.

The stress tensor is an example of a tensor which shows up in physical systems, and it's a really nice visualization of what a tensor might physically be. When you imagine it as a transformation on a vector - it takes in the normal vector for a surface in the fluid, and gives the force on the surface.

## Statistical Mechanics

The emergence of tensors in large collections of particles is by far my favorite way that they show up in physics. Consider a collection of n particles. Each of these will have a velocity vector. If I want to know the average speed of the particles, I would take the mean velocity,

$$<v>_i = \sum_{p=1}^{n}v_p,i$$

I might also want to know the variance in the particle velocities,

$$<v^2>_ij = \sum_{q=1}^{n} \sum_{p=1}^{n}v_{p,i} v_{q,i}$$

or even higher order moments like the third moment which would describe skew of the distribution. Together, all the moments of the velocity distribution would perfectly describe the velocity distribution of the collection of particles. Notice that the mean velocity (the first moment) is a vector, the second moment is a symmetric 2nd order tensor, and the higher moments are higher order tensors. This shows how stress tensors emerge from large collections of particles and is in some way an alternative derivation of the stress tensor when you recognize the correspondence between velocity, momentum and force.
